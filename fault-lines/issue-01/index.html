Sanctions · Shadow fleet

The shadow fleet stopped hiding. Your red flags need to catch up.

Russia's tankers have swapped false flags for their own. Here is what that means for the money trail, and for the controls that are meant to catch it.

The 60-second version: follow one cargo from Novorossiysk to your payments queue.69 s · captions on screen

Welcome to the first issue of Fault Lines.

After many years working in financial crime, I've seen the same thing again and again: the biggest risks don't start in a transaction monitoring alert. They start on a map, with a war, a sanctions package or a new trade route, and they reach your bank months later as a payment that looks perfectly ordinary. Each fortnight I'll take one of those stories, follow the money, and finish with red flags you can use.

We start at sea.

01The story

Since 2022, Western governments have tried to squeeze Russia's oil revenue with sanctions and a price cap, enforced through the Western services the tanker trade depends on: insurance, finance and shipping. Russia's answer was a parallel fleet: older tankers, often bought second-hand through newly formed companies, insured outside the mainstream market and registered under flags with light oversight.

Governments have been listing these ships one by one. The EU's list passed 673 vessels with its 21st package on 23 July 2026. The UK has now designated more than 600, adding 12 more on 8 October. The US has focused its recent maritime action on Iran, designating 17 more vessels in Iran's shadow fleet on 8 October. Separately, Washington has said it is weighing a partial easing that would let sanctioned tankers carry non-sanctioned oil, to bring down freight rates. No decision had been announced when this issue went out.

The bigger shift is in tactics. For years, the classic tell was the false flag: a tanker claiming a registry that didn't recognise it. According to the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA), falsely flagged tankers carried only 2% of the shadow fleet's Russian oil in September 2026, down from 24% a year earlier. Russian-flagged tankers' share rose from 9% to 36%, making Russia's own flag the largest in the shadow fleet.

The fleet is moving from hiding to openly flying the Russian flag, and protection at sea is following: European boardings have become harder in some waters.

02The money trail

A sanctioned cargo still has to be loaded, insured, sold and paid for, and each step touches the financial system:

  1. Ownership. The tanker sits in a single-ship company, often recently incorporated in a third country, with management passed between small operators.
  2. Insurance. Cover comes from outside the International Group of P&I Clubs, or can't be verified at all.
  3. The voyage. Cargo may change hands at sea through ship-to-ship (STS) transfers, which can blend or relabel its origin.
  4. The sale. Trading companies with short histories buy and resell the cargo, sometimes several times.
  5. The payment. Funds route through banks in third countries, often in non-dollar currencies, with costs such as freight or "services" inflated to keep the headline price under the cap on paper.

Banks touch this chain through trade finance, correspondent payments, ship finance, insurance, freight and port services. Most of the time they never see a tanker. They see a payment.

03Red flags for Monday morning

  1. Old ship, new owner. A tanker of 15 years or more bought recently by a newly formed single-ship company.
  2. Flag history. Frequent flag changes, a registry with weak oversight, or a flag that doesn't match registry records. Check IMO GISIS or Equasis rather than trusting what the ship broadcasts. New in 2026: a Russian flag is now a signal in its own right, not just a missing false one.
  3. Dark or impossible voyages. AIS gaps or spoofed positions near loading ports or known STS areas. Allow for GPS interference in conflict zones before drawing conclusions.
  4. Ship-to-ship transfers. STS in open water, at night, or several per cargo, especially when the counterpart vessel also has gaps in its track.
  5. Unverifiable insurance. Cover outside the International Group, or an insurer you can't confirm exists.
  6. Documents that don't add up. A bill of lading or origin that conflicts with the voyage history; price-cap attestations paired with unusually high freight or ancillary costs.
  7. Payments with no trade logic. Third-country intermediaries with no link to the cargo, round-tripping, or newly opened accounts settling large energy trades in non-dollar currencies.

No single flag proves evasion. The pattern does.

04The AI angle

Shadow-fleet detection is a data-fusion problem. The evidence sits in different places: vessel tracking data, ownership registries, trade documents and payment messages. Human teams rarely join them up in time.

This is where AI helps:

  • Voyage anomaly detection spots loitering, dark periods and implausible routes across thousands of ships.
  • Entity resolution links shell owners, managers and payment counterparties that share addresses, directors or ships.
  • Document checking. Language models can read bills of lading, attestations and invoices and flag contradictions with the voyage data.
  • Network analysis traces a payment back to a vessel, and a vessel back to an owner on a sanctions list.

The catch is explainability. A model that says "suspicious" isn't enough for a SAR or a sanctions decision. The analyst still needs the story, and that's what good tools should give them.

05Watchlist

  • Washington: whether talk of easing shadow-fleet sanctions becomes policy, and what licensing would look like.
  • LNG: the UK's latest Russia package reached into the LNG shadow fleet. Gas may be the next frontier.
  • The Baltic: the legal limits on boarding tankers in narrow straits, and whether more states stop trying.
